A first mixing pass

A good mix starts before any plugin is loaded. Name every track, colour the groups, and delete the takes you already know you will not use. Ten quiet minutes of housekeeping saves an hour of hunting for the right fader later.

Set levels first

Pull every fader down and bring the tracks back one at a time, starting with whatever carries the song. Aim to leave plenty of headroom on the master bus. If the balance works with volume and panning alone, the later processing has an easy job.

Check the balance at a low listening level. Quiet playback hides the room and shows which parts disappear. A vocal that vanishes at low volume is usually too soft, whatever it sounds like when loud.

Carve space with equalisation

Two instruments that live in the same range will mask each other. Rather than boosting both, cut a little from the one that matters less. High pass filters on parts with no useful low end clear mud that is hard to hear on any single track.

Sweep carefully and listen in context. A sound that seems thin when soloed often sits perfectly once the rest of the arrangement returns.

Control dynamics

Compression evens out performances, but too much of it flattens them. Set the threshold so the meter moves on the loud phrases and rests on the quiet ones. A slower attack lets transients through and keeps drums lively.

Automation is often the better tool. Riding a fader through the chorus keeps the natural feel that a heavy compressor would remove.

Add depth last

Send several tracks to one shared reverb so they sound like they occupy the same room. Short delays can widen a part without washing it out. Filter the returns, since low frequencies in a reverb tail cloud the whole mix.

Take a break

Ears adapt quickly. Step away, then compare your mix with a reference track at matched loudness. Write down what differs, fix the two largest problems, and stop. A finished mix teaches more than an endless one.
